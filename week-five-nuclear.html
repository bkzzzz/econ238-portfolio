The same reactor, a different financing bill

How much can the cost of electricity change when a nuclear plant takes longer to build or costs more to finance?

Bingkun Ye (Brank) · Environmental Economics · October 2026

The model gives a cost of about $80/MWh with a 3% cost of capital and four years of spending before opening. At 10% and ten years, it rises to $212/MWh. The plant's construction budget and electricity output are the same in both cases.

Why the costs change

The plant does not earn money from electricity sales while it is being built. A longer schedule means that money already spent has to be financed for longer. A higher cost of capital also raises the return needed once the plant opens. Try setting the rate to 0%: in this model, extending the schedule then leaves the cost per MWh unchanged.

The construction budget is only part of the cost. For a project with a large upfront investment, the financing terms and the time until opening also matter.

A government guarantee could lower investors' financing costs, but taxpayers would take on some of the risk. Faster construction could reduce costs too. This calculation does not tell us how much safety review is worth, or value the electricity and emissions reductions lost during a delay. Those would have to be included in a policy comparison.

Sources and calculation

The construction and operating costs come from EIA's January 2024 contractor study. Its advanced-nuclear reference case uses $7,861/kW for construction before financing, $156.20/kW-year for fixed operating costs, $2.52/MWh for variable operating costs, and a 40-year operating life. The reference plant is 2,156 MW; the capital totals shown here are scaled to 1 GW.

The 85% capacity factor comes from the IEA/NEA 2020 comparison. Fuel is held at an assumed $8/MWh. Equal annual payments and their timing are also assumptions. EIA lists 52 months for physical construction and 84 months for total lead time; the slider changes the period over which this model spends the money.

Formula and assumptions

K is construction cost per kW before financing, r is the annual real cost of capital, T is the spending period, N is the operating life, and CF is the capacity factor. Each annual payment is made at the year's midpoint.

Opening capital = (K/T) × Σ[j = 0 to T−1] (1+r)^(T−j−0.5)
Capital recovery factor = r / [1 − (1+r)^(−N)]
At r = 0, recovery factor = 1/N
Annual output per kW = 8.76 × CF MWh
Generation cost = (opening capital × recovery factor + fixed O&M) / annual output + variable O&M + fuel

The model uses the same real rate during construction and operation. It excludes inflation, taxes, subsidies, cost overruns, salvage value, transmission charges, and a separate decommissioning allowance. The result is a simplified generation cost. The capital total at opening includes the required return on earlier spending; it is not an actual project's debt balance.

Checking the result and taking it further

The 0% case checks that construction time alone does not add financing costs when the real rate is zero. The results were also checked against a separate calculation that discounts each payment and each year's electricity output.

A next step would be to use a project's actual payment schedule. A delay after most of the money has been spent could be more expensive than the evenly spread schedule used here. It would also be useful to compare who bears that risk under different financing arrangements.
