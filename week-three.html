Why Does Building Clean Energy Take So Long?

Bingkun Ye (Brank) · ECON 238 · Week Three

Why is it so hard to build infrastructure in the U.S.? A friend and I discuss environmental review, lawsuits, and whether giving officials more freedom would help. NEPA, the National Environmental Policy Act, is our starting point.

Synthesis

In our conversation, my friend said that some legal challenges are useful because they catch real problems. I agreed, but it made my suggestion of faster review less straightforward. If a neighborhood needs time to discover a problem with a project, cutting that time is a cost too. I still want decisions to come sooner. I just would not judge the change only by how many months it saves.

Potter helps explain why review gets so long: agencies worry about defending it in court. Doomberg thinks this is part of a much wider problem with litigation. That raises a question for Howard's proposal to give officials more responsibility: what stops the same fights from moving to another permit? Wang's comparison with China adds another concern. Building quickly matters, but people should still have a way to challenge what gets built around them. I do not think fewer lawyers, by itself, answers either problem.

One practical change I would support is a public timeline showing who is responsible for each unfinished part of a project. There are already lead-agency rules and review deadlines in the law, which survived the removal of CEQ's regulations. The timeline would also show financing and grid-connection delays. An agency would have to explain why it needed more time, and a developer could not blame review when it lacked funding. If lenders could see more clearly what was holding a project up, some might be more willing to finance it.

My friend also worried about giving officials too much power. That is why I would keep public comments and a way to challenge unlawful decisions. A family living beside a proposed transmission line may gain little from cheaper electricity elsewhere. Their objections deserve an answer even if the project is worth building. I would also worry about staff rushing unfinished work just to meet a deadline.

We suggested that local institutions can earn trust because people see their results. Gallup's June 2026 survey found 67% confidence in small business, but that does not establish a new institution everyone trusts. A small group's shared interests are harder to reproduce on a large project. Social media makes mistakes visible; agencies need to show how they respond to them.

I would not assume this is an inevitable result of becoming richer. There are specific problems to fix. A faster permit is little help if a project still cannot connect to the grid. What I would want to see is earlier operation without more serious environmental damage. If shorter reviews repeatedly missed harm, I would reconsider how much speed was worth.
