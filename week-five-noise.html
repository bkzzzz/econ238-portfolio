A quieter park. Whose street gets louder?

What happens to the nearby residential street when traffic is diverted away from a park?

Bingkun Ye (Brank) · Environmental Economics · October 2026

Redirect half the park-side traffic in this scenario: the park gets about 3 dB quieter, while homes along the receiving road get about 7.7 dB louder. The same 500 vehicles enter a street that started with far less traffic.

Try diverting some of the traffic

This is a hypothetical example, not a noise measurement in Rochester. It compares outdoor sound at the park and beside the residential road over one hour. Why the two changes are different

Removing 500 vehicles from the park road cuts its traffic in half. Adding those cars to a residential road with only 100 vehicles/hour raises its traffic to six times the original amount. That is why the change at the homes is larger. With similar vehicles, halving sound energy lowers the level by about 3 dB. FHWA guidance puts the change associated with half the perceived loudness at about 10 dB.

Rerouting helps the park in this example, but adds noise beside homes and extra travel time. Whether it is worth doing depends on how people value those changes.

Our EETT discussion raised the idea of moving the noise somewhere else. This example follows that idea to the street receiving the traffic. Before choosing a route, I would want to know who lives there and when the noise occurs. Noise during recreation and noise when someone is trying to sleep may have different costs. A barrier or a more distant route could help, but it would also cost money or add travel time.

How the noise calculation works

The model treats each vehicle as contributing the same average sound energy at a given location. The coefficient is set to produce 70 dBA of traffic noise at 1,000 vehicles/hour. A separate 45 dBA background is added. The residential road uses the same coefficient for its original traffic. The noise-reduction slider applies only to the diverted cars.

Formula and assumptions
f = redirected share; A = extra noise reduction in dB
c = 10,000 relative energy units per vehicle/hour
B = 10^(45/10)
Park level = 10 log10[c × 1,000 × (1−f) + B]
Home level = 10 log10[c × (100 + 1,000 × f × 10^(−A/10)) + B]
Extra vehicle-hours = 1,000 × f × extra minutes / 60

The result is an average outdoor sound level over one hour. This simple calculation leaves out differences in trucks, acceleration, congestion, buildings, terrain, and indoor shielding. It also leaves out extra emissions along a longer route and changes in the number of trips. It is not a site-specific FHWA Traffic Noise Model calculation.

The population counts do not mean that everyone hears the plotted level. Decibels are not added across neighborhoods, and the model does not estimate health effects or a total dollar cost. Those would require more information about actual exposure and people's responses.

Checking the result and taking it further

With no rerouting, the sound levels stay at their starting values and there is no added travel time. The traffic total also stays at 1,100 vehicles/hour under every setting. Increasing the noise reduction for diverted traffic lowers the added sound at homes.

A next step would be to measure both routes during the same hour, count vehicle types and speeds, and estimate the detour time. Residents and park users could then be asked how much the changes matter to them. It would also be useful to compare rerouting with reducing vehicle noise at its source.
