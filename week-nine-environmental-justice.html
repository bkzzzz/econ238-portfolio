01 / QUIET

An improvement has a price.

A cleaner or quieter neighborhood becomes more desirable. When housing is scarce, that demand can enter the price of living there. This is one reason to ask who owns the asset before calling an improvement a distributional success.

The 6.8% estimate describes the study's treatment effect close to barriers. It does not establish how much an individual tenant's rent will change, or how much of the benefit existing residents will retain. Read the working paper.

02 / STAYING

Quiet streets, rising rents

Capitalization can reward owners while making a neighborhood harder to enter or remain in. The following are economic mechanisms to investigate, rather than measured outcomes for a particular neighborhood.

Who could retain the improvement?
PersonA possible gainThe unresolved question
Resident homeownerQuieter living and a more valuable asset.Can the household afford ongoing taxes and maintenance? An unrealized gain does not pay a current bill.
Renter at lease renewalQuieter living while able to remain.How much will future rent absorb, and is another affordable home available nearby?
Absentee landlordA higher asset value and potentially higher future rents.How much public spending reaches the residents rather than the owner?
TaxpayerThe benefits of a project that may reduce an external cost.Do the gains justify construction and upkeep, compared with another use of the money?

A design worth testing: pair environmental improvements with additional housing and targeted support for current vulnerable residents. Tenant assistance, affordable leases, or shared-equity ownership could preserve more of the benefit. Each needs funding, eligibility rules, and evidence about who actually stays.

The test

Track the people who lived there before the project. A neighborhood's rising average income does not tell us whether its original residents are better off.

03 / CONTROL

What changes with tribal control?

A land acknowledgment can explain a site's history without changing who manages it. Compare three arrangements for a conservation site and the rights each would provide.

Choose an arrangement. These are design scenarios, not measured outcomes or a ranking.
Scenario A / Recognition

The statement changes. The authority stays.

Assume the institution names a tribe's historical relationship to the site, but makes no accompanying contractual, budgetary, or title change.

Who decides?

The existing owner or public agency retains its legal decision rights.

Who receives income?

The existing revenue arrangements continue. The statement alone transfers no income.

What remains unresolved?

Whether greater awareness leads to action; who can enforce any promise; whether recognition substitutes for a costly commitment.

Scenario B / Shared authority

Give the partnership a budget and a vote.

Assume the parties negotiate a funded agreement giving a tribal government specified management powers, rather than an advisory invitation alone.

Who decides?

The agreement allocates tribal and agency authority over matters such as access, restoration, and staffing. Dispute rules must be explicit.

Who receives income?

The agreement specifies operating funds and any revenue shares. Tribal participation does not by itself determine who keeps fees.

What remains unresolved?

Budget durability, divided responsibilities, veto points, and whether the tribal partner has enough power to carry out its obligations.

Scenario C / Title and responsibility

Transfer the asset. Negotiate the obligations.

Assume a legally authorized transfer to the relevant tribal nation, with negotiated terms for conservation, public access, and the form of title.

Who decides?

The tribal owner governs within the applicable law and agreed covenants. Title form and any retained public powers matter.

Who receives income?

The owner can receive agreed site revenues and bears stewardship costs. The transfer must specify liabilities and continuing public funding.

What remains unresolved?

Competing claims, tribal consent, financing, conservation rules, visitor access, and the security of the transfer against future reversal.

These scenarios do not describe a universal tribal property regime. Tribes are governments with distinct legal histories and rights; they are not interchangeable conservation contractors.

Leasing authority

Under the HEARTH Act, a tribe with federally approved leasing regulations can approve covered surface leases without seeking separate federal approval for each one. The change concerns leasing authority; it does not transfer a national park or remove all land-use restrictions.2

04 / JUDGMENT

What would count as success?

The arrangement I would test: start with a limited site, real management and revenue rights, and enough funding to meet the duties. Agree access and repair responsibilities before transfer. After ten years, compare land condition, completed repairs, visitor access, and tribal income after costs with similar sites. The relevant nations should help choose the measures.

There are real models to study, with different rights and obligations. The 125-acre ’O Rew transfer to the Yurok Tribe pairs ownership with restoration and access. Te Urewera in New Zealand has its own legal identity and a board involving Tūhoe and the Crown. Neither case proves that transferring every national park would succeed.

For a renter, a better place matters if they can keep living there. For a tribal nation, recognition matters differently when it includes the power to choose what happens next.

Who benefits?

If public money raises a landlord's asset value, what would make the project a success for the tenant?

Who decides?

If a tribal government chooses a use outsiders dislike, how much autonomy were they prepared to recognize?

How would we know?

Ten years later, whose income, housing security, and decision rights would you measure?
